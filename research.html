Local Queens and Princesses: The Impact of Female Dynasts on Provision of Public Goods

PDF

Who gets elected to public office matters for the provision of public goods. Women leaders are often expected to bring something different to office, and in many settings they have been found to raise the provision of public goods. However, women who reach political office tend to come from political dynasties, and dynastic politicians have mostly been found to lower it. A female dynast therefore brings into office two identities that the literature associates with opposite effects. I study what happens to public goods when Indonesian districts, which are responsible for basic services, elect a female dynast. I combine district elections from 2005 to 2020 with eight waves of a national village census, and I estimate the effect in a staggered difference-in-differences design. Electing a female dynast raises an index of public infrastructure by 0.075 of a village standard deviation, mostly through household infrastructure and education. Comparing four kinds of leaders, most of the gain comes from the leader being a woman. The gain is concentrated among women from entrenched political families who did not take the seat directly from a relative, while men from the same families add almost nothing. Because women are far more likely than men to reach office through their families, understanding what women leaders deliver requires understanding the dynasties they come from.

Does Rent Beget Rent? Natural Resource Boom and Political Selection

How do economic shocks shape the entrenchment of political dynasties? I study Indonesia’s nickel boom—a large, location-specific rent shock driven by electric-vehicle battery demand and Chinese downstream investment—and its effect on the election of dynastic politicians in district-level office. Using an original dataset on Indonesian dynasties from 2005 to 2018 and an event-study design that exploits the staggered timing of a district’s first nickel permit, I show that a nickel opening is followed by a medium-run rise in dynastic selection on the order of six percentage points. The effect runs through dynastic persistence—the election of dynastic descendants. Falsification tests using coal and gold permits produce no analogous effect. Resource booms can entrench the political concentrations that already exist in the places that hold them.
